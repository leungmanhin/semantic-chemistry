# The Lantern-Keepers of Aelmere

Aelmere is a coastal village built into a horseshoe of grey cliffs above the Cold Sea. The village has stood for at least nine generations of Lantern-Keepers. Its sole industry, and its sole defense, is the tending of two hundred and forty stone lanterns spaced along the Cliff Path, the harbor wall, and the inland Hollows.

Each lantern burns a thick oil called mire-essence. Mire-essence gives off a pale silver light, called mist-light, that the villagers can see from many miles at sea. Lit lanterns are how the fishing fleet finds its way home through fog. Lit lanterns are also how Aelmere keeps the wraiths from rising out of the Sunken Cove.

The Lantern-Keepers are a hereditary order. There are forty-three Keepers in the present generation, organized under a Council of seven elders. The Council meets at every full moon in a small stone hall called the Watch. The Council decides how mire-essence reserves are allotted, who is permitted to apprentice, and which lantern-stations are to be re-staffed when a Keeper dies or retires.

Aelmere does not buy or trade for mire-essence. The villagers make their own, in a low building near the harbor called Cauldron Hall. The recipe is old and the ingredients are unusual. Mire-essence is distilled from silken threads that nightmoths leave on lantern rims, boiled in salt-water harvested from a cove pool, in copper cauldrons that have stood in Cauldron Hall for over a century. There is no other known source.

This creates a problem. A lantern must already be lit, with mire-essence, in order for nightmoths to come and leave threads. The Keepers do not remember a time when there was no mire-essence in the reserves, and they do not know how the first lanterns were lit. The Council's working theory is that the first Keepers found mire-essence in the Hollows long ago, and that the village has been recycling its supply ever since.

The recycling is fragile. A single bad winter, in which too many lanterns are lost to cold winds and not enough mire-essence is recovered from threads, can put the village a year behind. There are records in the Watch of three such winters in the last hundred and forty years. After each of them, the Sunken Cove produced wraiths within the next dark moon.

The current year is bad. Two unusual cold snaps have already extinguished sections of the Cliff Path lanterns. The salt-bloom harvest at the new moon was thinner than usual. The sky-cats have been seen on the spires fewer nights than the Keepers would prefer. And Coraline Ash, the newest apprentice, has begun asking questions that the senior Keepers find difficult to answer. The story below covers approximately a year and a quarter of village life, beginning two days before the autumn equinox and ending in early winter of the following year.
