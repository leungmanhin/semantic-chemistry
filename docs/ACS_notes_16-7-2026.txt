Probably the cleanest interpretation is to distinguish three related but separate ideas: structural closure, metabolic viability, and causal usefulness.

The core ACS closure condition should still be an endogenous rule-and-motif closure condition. I.e.,  words, the rules in the candidate ACS should produce semantic motifs or internally generated control states that, within a bounded number of steps, enable or catalyze rules in the same set again. The relevant loop doesn't have to exist entirely at the level of domain propositions, but it should exist somewhere inside the operating chemistry.

This means that both substrate-level and control-level closure are legit things..... 

-- A substrate-level loop is something like the biomedical example, where PersistentImmuneActivation eventually leads through a sequence of semantic relations back to PersistentImmuneActivation. 

-- A control-level loop might involve detecting a why-question, activating causal-alignment rules, constructing a successful explanation, producing an internal success trace or token allocation, and thereby making the same question-answering strategy more likely to activate again. 

--- A hybrid loop can alternate between semantic motifs and control motifs.

The important qualification is that the control feedback should be endogenous to the system. Internally generated attention shifts, chamber activations, reusable answer templates, learned gating criteria, or minted fuel tokens can all count as products or catalysts in the ACS.  But -- pure external reward by itself normally should not count as the thing that closes the loop. If the structure is simply “rule fires, answer is produced, external evaluator rewards the rule, rule fires again,” that is better described as a rewarded or subsidized rule rather than as an actual structurally closed ACS. The external reward is analogous to food or environmental selection: it can sustain and select an ACS, but it does not by itself make the ACS closed.

So I would say that the biomedical examples are intentionally showing genuine semantic-substrate closure, whereas in smaller QA examples closure may be more naturally visible at the control level. In a simple QA task, the semantic content usually flows from question to answer and does not literally reconstruct the original question. However, a reusable inference-control pattern can recur across many questions of the same type. In larger domains like biology, ecology, fictional-world dynamics, self-models, or social feedback, explicit semantic cycles may arise naturally as well.

It is also useful to separate closure from metabolic viability. A structurally closed loop might still die if it consumes more fuel, attention, or CPU than it earns. Metabolic viability means that, over repeated operation, the loop replenishes enough resources to cover its firing costs, rent, and decay. 

Causal usefulness is a further condition: ablating the loop should reduce task performance, explanation quality, novelty, or some other relevant outcome, and its contribution should not be entirely reproduced by redundant alternatives. 

So the strongest object of interest would be a self-recreating subnetwork that is structurally closed, metabolically viable, and causally useful.

Regarding the single-rule case --  yeah, a single-rule ACS is formally possible. Suppose one rule consumes an externally supplied food input together with an internal catalyst or control motif, and its firing regenerates that same internal catalyst. Then the one rule genuinely closes a self-loop and can recur. This is analogous to a one-reaction autocatalytic system in actual chemistry.

However, a single rule that merely earns external reward whenever it produces a correct answer should not automatically be called an ACS. Unless the rule regenerates some internal non-food condition required for its own future operation, it is just a useful rule being paid by the environment.

But genuine singleton ACSs -- though possible -- are usually not the most interesting scientific case. They have no mutual enablement among distinct rules, little or no group-level synergy, and little emergent organization beyond an ordinary persistent rule. They are kinda edge cases,  though they coudl also be sometimes seeds from which larger ACSs can develop. I would therefore allow them formally, perhaps calling them singleton ACSs, while treating composite ACSs with two or more mutually supporting reactions as the main objects of interest.

A concise revised definition could be something like: an ACS is structurally closed when every non-food semantic or control motif required to sustain its reactions is produced or regenerated by the set itself within a bounded number of steps, yielding a recurrent component in the rule-motif graph. It is metabolically viable when its earned resources cover its firing costs, rent, and decay. It is causally useful when activating or ablating it measurably changes downstream utility.

So the answer is that both substrate-level and broader control-level closure are allowed, but external reward alone should not be allowed to trivialize the definition.
